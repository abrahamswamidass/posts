When the World Ended

Five times in Earth's history, life was nearly erased. Each time it came back stranger, bolder, and more complex than before. This is that story.

Earth Forms
A disk of gas and dust around a young Sun collapses. Rocky planetesimals collide over millions of years. The surface is molten — a magma ocean. The Moon forms when a Mars-sized body (Theia) strikes and blasts debris into orbit.

4,100 – 3,800 million years ago
Late Heavy Bombardment
The inner solar system is pelted by asteroids and comets — possibly 20,000 impact craters on Earth. The oceans form from comet delivery and volcanic outgassing. Remarkably, microbial life may have survived in deep hydrothermal vents.

3,700 million years ago
First Life — Single-Celled Organisms
The oldest confirmed evidence of life: stromatolites (layered microbial mats) preserved in ancient rock. These prokaryotes — ancestors of all bacteria — have no nucleus, no organelles. Just a membrane, DNA, and an astonishing drive to replicate.

2,700 million years ago
Cyanobacteria Invent Photosynthesis
Cyanobacteria evolve oxygenic photosynthesis — splitting water molecules and releasing oxygen as waste. This seemingly small chemistry experiment will change everything. For the anaerobic life that dominates Earth at this moment, it is a death sentence.

Proterozoic — 2,500 to 541 Ma
2,400 million years ago
Great Oxidation Event — The First Mass Die-Off
Oxygen accumulates in the atmosphere for the first time. For 90% of Earth's existing microbial life — all of which evolved to breathe without oxygen — free O₂ is a deadly poison. The anaerobic world collapses. Earth's first great extinction is caused not by an asteroid or volcano, but by breathing.

Eukaryotes — The Cell With a Nucleus
A prokaryote engulfs another and instead of digesting it, keeps it alive as a power source — the mitochondrion. This is endosymbiosis, and it produces the first complex cell with a nucleus. Every plant, animal, and fungus alive today descends from this one moment of unlikely cooperation.

717 – 635 million years ago
Snowball Earth
Ice sheets advance from the poles to the equator — possibly covering the entire ocean surface. Average temperatures drop to −50°C. Life retreats to hydrothermal vents and thin ice-free patches near volcanos. After tens of millions of years, volcanic CO₂ accumulates and melts the ice in a catastrophic greenhouse rebound.

600 million years ago
Ediacaran Life — First Multicellular Animals
After Snowball Earth, life explodes into new forms. The Ediacaran biota: soft-bodied organisms that look like nothing alive today. Some resemble fronds, discs, tubes. They have no mouths, no eyes, no predators. The ocean floor is a quiet garden. It will not last.

Paleozoic — 541 to 252 Ma
541 million years ago
Cambrian Explosion — The Body Plan Revolution
Within 25 million years, every major animal body plan that exists today appears in the fossil record. Eyes evolve for the first time. Predation begins. Hard shells and skeletons erupt across the tree of life — partly as armor against the new danger of being eaten. The arms race that defines animal evolution starts here.

End-Ordovician Extinction — #1 of the Big Five
Gondwana drifts over the South Pole. Ice caps grow. Sea levels drop by 100 metres, draining shallow continental seas where most marine life lives. Then the ice melts rapidly, flooding warmer anoxic water back over the reefs. Two pulses of extinction: 85% of all species gone. Trilobites, brachiopods, graptolites — whole groups wiped out.

Plants Colonise Land
Green algae evolve to survive out of water. The first land plants — mosses and liverworts — transform bare rock into soil, pumping oxygen into the atmosphere and drawing down CO₂. Land, empty for billions of years, suddenly turns green.

Late Devonian Extinction — #2 of the Big Five
A prolonged crisis rather than a single event. Land plants spread across continents, their roots breaking down rock and pumping nutrients into the ocean — causing algal blooms that strip oxygen from the water. Reef ecosystems collapse. The warm shallow seas that filled with extraordinary Devonian fish life go dark. 75% of species lost over perhaps 20 million years. Land vertebrates — fish learning to walk — survive the oceans' collapse by leaving.

385 million years ago
The First Trees
Archaeopteris, the first true tree, rises 30 metres high. Forests spread across the land. They are so efficient at pulling CO₂ from the atmosphere that global temperatures plunge — possibly contributing to the Devonian extinction itself. Trees remake the planet's chemistry before anything has adapted to them.

First Reptiles — The Amniote Egg
Vertebrates evolve the amniotic egg — a self-contained life-support system that can develop on dry land without water. Reptiles are freed from dependence on ponds and streams. They colonise the continental interior. Life conquers the land completely.

252 million years ago
End-Permian Extinction — "The Great Dying"
The worst day in the history of complex life. The Siberian Traps — a volcanic province the size of Australia — erupt for a million years, releasing enough CO₂ and SO₂ to warm the oceans by 10°C, acidify the seas, and strip the upper ocean of oxygen. 96% of all marine species vanish. 70% of land vertebrate species. Coral reefs disappear for 10 million years. The ocean floor goes silent. It takes 5–10 million years for ecosystems to recover.
Species lost · ~96% — the worst ever recorded

Mesozoic — 252 to 66 Ma
240 million years ago
First Dinosaurs
In the aftermath of the Great Dying, archosaurs rise to dominate the land. Within 10 million years, the first true dinosaurs appear — bipedal, fast, efficient breathers. Their hollow bones and air-sac respiratory system, shared with modern birds, give them a crucial metabolic edge in the low-oxygen Triassic world.

201 million years ago
End-Triassic Extinction — #4 of the Big Five
The supercontinent Pangaea begins splitting apart. The Central Atlantic Magmatic Province (CAMP) erupts — a volcanic event that covers 11 million km² with lava and floods the atmosphere with CO₂. 76% of species are lost. Crucosaurs, large amphibians, and most early archosaur lineages vanish. The survivors? Small, adaptable dinosaurs. They now inherit an empty world.

200 million years ago
First Mammals
Tiny, shrew-like, nocturnal. Warm-blooded. Fur. Feeding young with milk. Mammals survive the end-Triassic by being small and staying out of sight. For 135 million years they remain in the shadows of the dinosaurs, waiting.

130 million years ago
Flowering Plants — Angiosperms
Flowers appear — and with them, a new evolutionary contract. Insects carry pollen between plants in exchange for nectar. Fruit-bearing trees enlist birds and mammals to disperse seeds in exchange for food. Life invents mutualism at scale. The diversity of both insects and mammals explodes in the resulting co-evolution.
Yixian Formation, Liaoning, ChinaCretaceous deposits, Portugal & USA
66 million years ago
K-Pg Extinction — Asteroid Ends the Age of Dinosaurs
A 10-km asteroid strikes the Yucatán Peninsula at 72,000 km/h. The impact releases energy equivalent to a billion nuclear bombs. Ejecta re-entering the atmosphere ignites wildfires globally. A dust cloud blocks sunlight for years. Temperatures plummet. 76% of species — including all non-avian dinosaurs — are gone within decades. Only birds (the surviving dinosaur lineage), small mammals, and a few reptiles make it through. The Deccan Traps volcanism in India adds further poison to an already dying world.

Mammal Diversification — The Age of Mammals
With the dinosaurs gone, mammals explode into every available ecological niche. Whales evolve from land mammals that return to the sea. Bats take the skies. Horses, elephants, primates, rodents — the modern mammal families all appear within 10 million years. The survivors inherit the world.

Antarctica Freezes — Ice Ages Begin
South America and Australia separate from Antarctica, allowing the Antarctic Circumpolar Current to form. Antarctica is isolated from warm ocean currents and begins to glaciate. CO₂ levels fall as the Himalayas rise and weather silicate rock. The planet enters a long cooling trend that will culminate in the Pleistocene ice ages.

Human Time — Last 4 Million Years
4 million years ago
Hominids Walk Upright — Australopithecus
In the African savannah, a lineage of apes begins walking on two legs. Bipedalism frees the hands and begins the long feedback loop: tools enable better food, better food enables larger brains, larger brains enable better tools. The African rift valley — where tectonic forces are splitting the continent and changing the climate — becomes the cradle of human evolution.

2.6 Ma – 11,700 years ago
Pleistocene Ice Ages & Megafauna Collapse
Ice ages cycle every 100,000 years. Between the glaciations: woolly mammoths, cave bears, giant ground sloths, sabre-toothed cats, giant wombats. As the last ice age ends (~12,000 ya), these megafauna disappear continent by continent — the pattern tracks the arrival of Homo sapiens. Africa, where humans evolved alongside these animals, loses the fewest. The Americas and Australia, which had no prior contact, lose the most.

Homo sapiens — Modern Humans Appear
Anatomically modern humans emerge in Africa. For 200,000 years we share the planet with Neanderthals, Denisovans, and perhaps other Homo species. We interbreed — today's humans carry 1–4% Neanderthal DNA. By 40,000 years ago, we are the only Homo species remaining. No one knows exactly why.

Out of Africa — Global Migration
A small group of humans — perhaps a few thousand — crosses from Africa into the Arabian Peninsula. They follow coastlines, reach India, Southeast Asia, and Australia by 65,000 ya. Europe is settled by 45,000 ya. The Americas are reached via Beringia around 15,000 ya. Within 55,000 years, Homo sapiens inhabits every landmass except Antarctica.

3,300 – 1,200 BCE & 1,200 BCE – 550 CE
Bronze Age & Iron Age
Humans learn to smelt copper, then bronze, then iron. Metal tools transform agriculture, warfare, and trade. Cities rise along rivers — the Nile, Tigris-Euphrates, Indus, Yellow River. Writing emerges independently in Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, and Mesoamerica. Civilisations connect for the first time into trade networks spanning thousands of kilometres. The human footprint on the land grows irreversible.

1800 – Present
The Sixth Extinction — Now
Current species extinction rates are 100 to 1,000 times the natural background rate. The IUCN Red List tracks 44,000+ species as threatened. Amphibians are collapsing first — 41% of species threatened. Insects are declining at 2–3% per year in many regions. Vertebrate populations have fallen 73% on average since 1970. The cause is not an asteroid. It is not a volcano. It is land conversion, hunting, invasive species, climate change, and pollution — all driven by one species. The question of whether this becomes the sixth mass extinction may be decided within this century.
