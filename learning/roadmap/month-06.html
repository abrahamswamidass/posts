Core focus

Stop building pipelines. Start defending platforms.

For five months you've been earning vocabulary and reps. This month the altitude changes: nobody is going to ask you to hand-tune a dbt model in the interview that matters — they'll draw a box on a whiteboard and ask you to fill it in, then attack every choice you make. The skill now is argument. Knowing the patterns, knowing where each one breaks, and having the nerve to say "you don't need that here."

The architecture debate

Three answers dominate every "how should we organize our data platform" conversation. You need to hold all three in your head and know their failure modes cold.

  • Centralized warehouse / lakehouse — one platform team owns ingestion, modeling, and serving. What it solves: consistency, a single source of truth, easy governance, low coordination cost. Where it fails: the central team becomes a bottleneck, domain knowledge gets lost in translation, and the backlog grows faster than the team. This is what you actually run at Cardinal Health, and it's the right default for most orgs.
  • Data mesh — a socio-technical pattern, not a product. Four principles: domain-oriented ownership (the team that produces the data owns its pipelines), data-as-a-product (each dataset has an owner, an SLA, docs, and quality guarantees like any API), a self-serve data platform (central infra team builds the paved road so domains don't reinvent it), and federated computational governance (global standards enforced automatically, decisions made by a cross-domain guild). What it solves: the central-team bottleneck at real scale. Where it fails: it demands organizational maturity most companies don't have — you get a mess of inconsistent, ungoverned domain silos with none of the promised autonomy.
  • Data fabric — a metadata-driven integration layer that sits over your existing sources and stitches them together with active metadata, a knowledge graph, and automated discovery/lineage. What it solves: giving a unified access and governance plane over data that physically lives in many places, without a full re-platform. Where it fails: it's vendor-heavy, the "AI-driven automation" is often marketing, and it can paper over an unmodeled swamp instead of fixing it.

The honest take — and the thing that signals seniority in the room — is that most orgs don't need a mesh. Mesh is an answer to a scaling and organizational problem that a 200-person company simply does not have. If your platform team can keep up with demand, centralization wins on every axis that matters: cost, consistency, governance, speed. Reach for mesh when the bottleneck is organizational, not technical — and say exactly that when someone waves the buzzword at you.

Governance & security

This is your unfair advantage. You've lived healthcare data, PII, and access management — most engineers treat governance as an afterthought and it shows. Own the vocabulary and you're the person in the room who's actually shipped it under HIPAA.

  • Data catalog & discovery — the searchable inventory of what data exists, what it means, who owns it, and where it came from. Without one, governance is theater. This is the backbone the fabric and mesh patterns both lean on.
  • RBAC vs ABAC — Role-Based Access Control grants permissions to roles ("pharmacists can read the dispensing mart"). Attribute-Based Access Control decides at query time from attributes of the user, resource, and context ("allow if user.region == row.region AND data.classification != restricted"). RBAC is simpler and enough for most; ABAC is what you reach for when access depends on the data itself — which in healthcare, it constantly does.
  • PII classification & masking — you can't protect what you haven't classified. Tag columns as public / internal / confidential / PII / PHI, then apply data masking (dynamic redaction at read time) or tokenization (irreversible-without-the-vault substitution) so that a data scientist sees ***-**-4821 while the billing job sees the real SSN.
  • Row- and column-level security — the enforcement primitives. Column-level security hides or masks specific columns per role; row-level security filters which rows a principal can even see (a facility manager sees only their facility). Both belong in the warehouse, declared as policy, not bolted on in the BI tool.
  • HIPAA / GDPR obligations — HIPAA governs PHI (minimum-necessary access, audit logging, BAAs, breach notification); GDPR adds data-subject rights, purpose limitation, and the right to erasure. The engineering consequence is the same: everything must be classified, access must be least-privilege and audited, and you must be able to prove it after the fact.
  • Lineage as a governance tool — column-level lineage isn't just for debugging. It answers "which downstream tables are contaminated by this PII column?" and "if we delete this subject, where does their data flow?" — impact analysis and erasure become tractable instead of terrifying.
  • Access reviews — periodic recertification that every grant is still justified. This is exactly your Okta / Foundry access-management world: entitlements drift, people change teams, and a quarterly review catches the standing grants nobody remembers issuing. Name it as a control, not a chore.

Cost as architecture (FinOps)

Junior engineers optimize cost after the bill scares someone. Senior engineers design for it on day one and can point to the exact lever. In the cloud, cost is an architectural property — treat it like latency or reliability.

  • Storage vs compute — the fundamental split of the modern stack. Storage is cheap and roughly linear; compute is where the money burns. Decoupling them (object store + elastic engines) is the whole reason lakehouses exist. Most runaway bills are a compute problem wearing a storage costume.
  • The levers — partitioning and clustering so queries scan gigabytes instead of terabytes; materialization strategy (view vs table vs incremental — recompute cost vs storage cost vs freshness); warehouse sizing and auto-suspend so idle compute stops billing; right-sizing clusters and pruning the 3am jobs nobody reads anymore.
  • Showback vs chargeback — showback reports each team's spend for visibility; chargeback actually bills it back to their budget. You can't do either without cost attribution tags on every warehouse, job, and dataset — which means tagging is an architecture decision, made early.
  • Designing for cost from day one — the grain of your marts, whether a table is incremental, how you partition, and whether serving hits the warehouse or a cheaper cache are all cost decisions disguised as modeling decisions. Make them consciously.

Designing a data platform

Here's the reference lakehouse you should be able to draw from memory and defend layer by layer. Interviewers don't want a product list — they want to see you reason about trade-offs at each hop.

  • Ingestion — batch (scheduled bulk loads, CDC from operational DBs) plus streaming (Kafka → the lake for near-real-time). Decide per-source: does this need to be fresh in seconds, or is nightly fine? Freshness costs money.
  • Storage — bronze / silver / gold on an object store, in an open table format (Delta or Iceberg) so you get ACID, schema evolution, and time travel over cheap files instead of locking data into one engine.
  • Transformation — dbt for SQL-shaped modeling, Spark for heavy or non-SQL work. This is where medallion layering, SCD logic, and your grain decisions live.
  • Orchestration & quality — Airflow or Dagster running the DAGs, with data-quality gates (freshness, volume, schema, not-null) that fail the pipeline before bad data reaches gold, not after.
  • Serving — the warehouse for BI, a semantic layer for consistent metrics, and an API or reverse-ETL path for operational consumers. Different consumers, different latency and cost profiles.

When you present this on a whiteboard, lead with the non-functional requirements and let them drive every box: throughput (how much data, how fast), latency (how fresh does serving need to be), reliability (what's the SLA, what happens on failure), cost (what's the budget and how do we attribute it), and governance (PII, access, audit, retention). State the NFRs first, then justify each layer against them. That ordering — requirements before components — is what separates a staff engineer from someone reciting a stack.

Hands-on challenge
Capstone · ~15 hours

Build and document an end-to-end lakehouse platform

This is Capstone I — the piece that fuses Months 1–5 into one thing you can put a public link to. Not a toy notebook; a documented platform with an architecture story a staff engineer would respect. Pick a domain you can speak to (a hospital pharmacy network fits your background perfectly).

  1. Ingest a public dataset two ways: a batch load (bulk file/table load) and a simulated stream (a small producer trickling records into Kafka or a queue, then landed to the lake).
  2. Land it bronze → silver → gold with dbt and/or Spark on a real open table format — Delta or Iceberg — so you get ACID and schema evolution, not just Parquet in a folder. Declare the grain and the SCD choices explicitly.
  3. Orchestrate with Airflow or Dagster, including data-quality gates that fail the run before bad data reaches gold (freshness, row-count, schema, null checks).
  4. Add one governance element — column-level PII masking or role-based access on a sensitive column. One real control, implemented and demonstrated, beats a paragraph of intentions.
  5. Write a professional README: an architecture diagram (Mermaid), your grain and SCD decisions stated plainly, and 2–3 ADRs capturing the real trade-offs you made (table format, batch-vs-stream boundary, materialization strategy).
  6. Publish it as a public GitHub repo. This is portfolio piece #1 — write the README for a hiring manager skimming it in 90 seconds.
Stretch — Add a simple cost analysis: measure bytes scanned by your queries, estimate a monthly spend at published warehouse rates, and write one paragraph on the top two levers you'd pull to cut it in half.
Learn it with Claude

Prompts that replace the textbook

Paste these into Claude one at a time. Push back, ask for diagrams, ask "why not the other way." Conversation beats reading.

Mesh vs fabric vs centralized, decided
You are a principal data architect mentoring a senior engineer. Explain the real differences between a data mesh, a data fabric, and a centralized warehouse/lakehouse — what each one actually solves and where each one breaks in practice. Render a single Mermaid diagram that contrasts the three topologies side by side. Then give me a blunt, opinionated answer to one question: which of these does a 200-person healthcare company actually need, and why? No fence-sitting — take a position and defend it.
Governance checklist for HIPAA/PII
Generate a practical data-governance checklist for a HIPAA/PII environment (healthcare analytics platform). Organize it under these headings: data catalog & discovery, PII/PHI classification, RBAC vs ABAC access control, masking & tokenization, column/row-level security, lineage, and periodic access reviews. For each item give me the control, why an auditor cares, and one concrete way to implement it in a lakehouse. Mark which controls are HIPAA-specific vs generally good hygiene. Keep it to something I could hand a team and act on.
Data-platform system-design interview
Run a data-platform system-design interview with me. Start by giving me one open prompt — "design the analytics platform for a hospital pharmacy network" — and then act as the interviewer. Probe my design one layer at a time, in this order: non-functional requirements first, then ingestion, storage, transformation, orchestration & quality, serving, and governance. Ask ONE layer at a time, wait for my answer, then critique it — name the trade-off I missed and push on the weakest choice before moving on. At the end, score me and tell me the two layers where my reasoning was thinnest.
Staff-engineer review of my capstone
Act as a staff data engineer reviewing a portfolio project. I'll paste my capstone architecture diagram (Mermaid) and README. Review them the way you'd review a real design doc from a peer, then list the 5 weakest points in priority order — the things that would make you doubt the author's seniority or that a real interviewer would attack first. For each, tell me specifically how to fix it. Be direct; I want the critique that makes this repo stronger, not encouragement.
ADR template + worked example
Give me a clean, reusable ADR (Architecture Decision Record) template — context, decision drivers, options considered, decision, consequences, status. Then write one fully worked example ADR for a real decision in my capstone: choosing the table format (Delta vs Iceberg vs plain Parquet) for the lakehouse. Weigh the options honestly against my drivers (ACID, schema evolution, engine lock-in, ecosystem maturity, cost), make a call, and state the consequences I'm accepting. Keep it to one page so I can drop it straight into my repo.
Terminology to own

Say these without hesitating

Data mesh
Domain-oriented, decentralized ownership of data-as-a-product on a self-serve platform. An org pattern, not a tool.
Data fabric
A metadata-driven integration layer that unifies access and governance over data that stays physically distributed.
Data-as-a-product
Treating each dataset like a product: an owner, an SLA, docs, and quality guarantees.
Federated governance
Global standards enforced automatically, with decisions made by a cross-domain guild rather than one central team.
Data catalog
Searchable inventory of what data exists, what it means, who owns it, and where it came from.
RBAC
Role-Based Access Control — permissions granted to roles, users mapped to roles.
ABAC
Attribute-Based Access Control — access decided at query time from user, resource, and context attributes.
Data masking
Redacting or obfuscating sensitive values at read time so unauthorized principals never see the raw PII/PHI.
FinOps
Treating cloud cost as an engineering discipline — attribution, accountability, and deliberate optimization.
Showback / chargeback
Reporting each team's spend for visibility (showback) vs actually billing it to their budget (chargeback).
Reference architecture
A canonical, defensible platform design you can draw from memory and adapt to a problem.
ADR
Architecture Decision Record — a short doc capturing a decision, its options, and its trade-offs.
Non-functional requirements
Throughput, latency, reliability, cost, governance — the constraints that drive every architectural box.
Done when
  • You have a public capstone repo running an end-to-end lakehouse — batch + stream in, bronze/silver/gold out.
  • Your README carries an architecture diagram and 2–3 ADRs a staff engineer would respect.
  • You've implemented one real governance control — column-level masking or role-based access — and can demo it.
  • You can whiteboard a full data-platform design and defend the trade-off at every layer, NFRs first.
  • You can compare mesh, fabric, and centralized on demand — and say which one an org actually needs, with a reason.
