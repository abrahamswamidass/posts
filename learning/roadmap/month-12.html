Core focus

Turning eleven months of vocabulary into an offer

You didn't spend a year learning trivia. You built a working command of how data and AI systems are designed, operated, and reasoned about — and every one of those terms is now ammunition for the two rooms that decide your next role: the system-design whiteboard and the behavioral interview. This month is about aiming that ammunition.

System design — the senior differentiator

Junior candidates get grilled on syntax; senior candidates get handed a blank canvas and a vague requirement. That canvas is where twenty years of shipping real systems finally outweighs any fresh grad — if you can run the interview with structure instead of wandering. Run every data or AI/LLM design prompt through the same beats:

  • Clarify requirements — functional (what does it do?) and non-functional (how fast, how big, how reliable, who's governed?). Never start drawing until you've pinned these down out loud.
  • Estimate scale — rows/day, QPS, payload size, read/write ratio, growth. Back-of-envelope numbers signal you've operated real systems, not toy ones.
  • Sketch the architecture — ingestion → storage → processing → serving. Name the components with the precise term (lakehouse, CDC, streaming vs batch, feature store, vector index).
  • Discuss trade-offs — throughput vs latency, cost vs freshness, consistency vs availability, reliability, and governance/PII. This is the beat where you shine; you've lived these trade-offs in healthcare where the stakes were real.
  • Handle follow-ups — the interviewer will poke: "now it's 10x the traffic," "now it has to be exactly-once," "now legal needs an audit trail." Answer with a change to the design, not a shrug.

Hit those five beats and the whiteboard stops being an exam and becomes a conversation between two people who've built things. The vocabulary from Months 1–11 is what lets you name each move as you make it.

Positioning: from "Java architect" to "data + AI engineer"

Your resume today tells a true story that recruiters will screen out, because it reads as a legacy-enterprise profile. The fix is not to erase twenty years — it's to re-narrate them in the language of the job you now want. Lead with the two capstones as recent, tangible proof, then let the career underneath supply the credibility.

  • Translate the past — the Foundry pipelines become "batch and streaming data platform work"; Airflow/Dataflow become "orchestrated ELT at scale"; Kafka becomes "event-driven streaming ingestion"; access management becomes "data governance and PII controls." Same work, modern words.
  • The headline — the LinkedIn line recruiters screen on. Not "Senior Java Architect" but something like "Data & AI Engineer · Lakehouse & LLM systems · 20 yrs building regulated, high-stakes platforms."
  • The summary — three tight sentences: what you build now, the depth you bring, the domain edge (healthcare/PII). Front-load the keywords an ATS and a human both scan for.

Behavioral & STAR

A long career is a liability in interviews only if you tell it as a monologue. Compress it into STAR stories — Situation, Task, Action, Result — each two minutes, each ending in a quantified outcome. Prepare 6–8 that, between them, cover the whole behavioral surface:

  • Leadership — you drove something without formal authority.
  • Conflict — you disagreed with someone and the outcome was better for it.
  • Failure — something broke, you owned it, you changed what caused it.
  • Impact — a result you can put a number on (latency, cost, revenue, incidents avoided).
  • Ambiguity — no clear requirements, and you made progress anyway.

The Result is where seniors win and lose. "Reduced pipeline reprocessing incidents by 80%" beats "improved reliability" every time. Dig up the numbers now, while you have quiet time to remember them.

The portfolio as proof

Claims are cheap; a repo is not. Your two capstones — the lakehouse platform (Phase 1) and the production AI system (Phase 2) — are the evidence that closes the credibility gap between "I read about this" and "I built this." Document them like a senior engineer would:

  • Architecture diagrams — one clean picture per capstone. An interviewer should grasp the system in fifteen seconds.
  • READMEs — what it does, why it's built this way, how to run it, what you'd do next.
  • ADRs — the Architecture Decision Records you've been writing all year; they prove you reason about trade-offs, not just wire tools together.
  • This roadmap itself — a public artifact of twelve months of self-direction. Few candidates can show that they can teach themselves an entire discipline on purpose.
  • A short write-up/blog of the journey — and a GitHub profile where the pinned repos are the interview before the interview.

Staying current forever

The role is the milestone, not the finish line — this field will keep moving, and your edge is staying fluent without drowning. Make Claude your standing learning partner: paste a release note, a paper abstract, a new term, and ask it to situate the thing against what you already know. Follow signal, not noise — a couple of high-quality newsletters, the papers everyone is actually citing, the release notes for the tools you run. Then keep it light: one hour a week to read one thing deeply and update one note. A lightweight habit you'll actually keep beats an ambitious one you'll abandon in a month.

Hands-on challenge
Launch · ~ongoing

Run the job-launch sprint

This is the capstone of capstones — not a thing to build but a campaign to run. Treat it like a sprint with a shipping deadline, because that's exactly what it is.

  1. Polish both capstone repos. Finish the READMEs, add a clean architecture diagram to each, drop in the ADRs, and pin both on your GitHub profile so they're the first thing anyone sees.
  2. Rewrite the resume + LinkedIn around the data+AI narrative, then paste both plus a target job description into Claude and have it critique them against that JD — keyword gaps, weak bullets, buried impact.
  3. Write 6–8 STAR stories from your career covering leadership, conflict, failure, impact, and ambiguity — each ending in a quantified result.
  4. Do THREE mock interviews with Claude — one data system design, one AI/LLM system design, one behavioral. Take the feedback, iterate, and run each again until the score rises.
  5. Build a target list and apply to a first batch of roles. Tailor the resume lightly to each; send them.
  6. Publish a short write-up of your 12-month journey. It's genuine signal, and it closes the loop on the year.
Stretch — Do a "reverse interview": have Claude play you answering a hard design or behavioral question while you play the interviewer probing the answer. Grading a great answer is the fastest way to internalize what one sounds like when it's your turn.
Learn it with Claude

Prompts that run your interview prep

Paste these into Claude one at a time. Answer out loud where you can, ask for the sharper phrasing, and rerun the mocks until the scores climb.

Full data system-design mock
Act as a staff-level interviewer running a data system-design interview. Give me ONE open design prompt (e.g. "design a near-real-time analytics platform for hospital medication events"). Let me drive. As I go, probe every layer — ingestion, storage, processing, serving, governance, PII — and throw at least two scale/consistency follow-ups ("now it's 10x traffic," "now it must be exactly-once"). Ask one thing at a time and wait for me. At the end, score me 1–10 on structure, scale estimation, trade-off reasoning, and communication, and list my three biggest gaps to drill.
Full AI/LLM system-design mock
Act as a senior AI engineering interviewer. Ask me to design a production LLM system — a RAG or agentic application serving real users. Let me drive the architecture (retrieval, chunking, vector store, orchestration, serving), then probe the parts candidates skip: evaluation strategy, cost per request, latency budget, guardrails, and failure modes. Add a follow-up on how I'd measure quality in production. One question at a time, wait for me. At the end, score me 1–10 across correctness, evals, cost/latency awareness, and safety, and tell me exactly what a stronger candidate would have said.
Rewrite my resume against a JD
I'll paste my current resume and a target job description. Rewrite my resume bullets to match that JD: lead with my two capstones, translate my legacy enterprise work (Foundry pipelines, Airflow/Dataflow, Kafka, access management) into modern data+AI language, quantify impact wherever possible, and surface the keywords this JD and an ATS will screen for. Flag any bullet that reads as "legacy Java" and give me a data+AI framing instead. Keep it honest — no invented numbers; where I'm missing a metric, ask me for it. [Then I paste resume + JD.]
Career story → polished STAR
I'll describe a real experience from my career in rough form. Turn it into a crisp STAR story — Situation, Task, Action, Result — that runs about two minutes spoken, keeps me as the clear protagonist, and ends on a quantified result. If I didn't give you a number, ask me for one rather than inventing it. Then generate the 3 follow-up questions a sharp interviewer would most likely ask after that story, and a one-line note on what each is really testing.
30-day prep plan + the 20 terms
Given my weak spots — [I'll list them, e.g. "scale estimation, LLM evals, negotiating"] — build me a focused 30-day interview-prep plan: what to drill each week, which mocks to repeat, and one measurable checkpoint per week. Then give me the 20 most important terms from a 12-month data + AI engineering curriculum (dimensional modeling, streaming, orchestration, lakehouse, RAG, evals, agents, guardrails, and the rest) that I should be able to define in one sentence instantly — as a flashcard list, term on one side, one-line definition on the other.
Terminology to own

The final vocabulary self-test

Cover the definitions. If you can say each of these in one clean sentence — data, AI, and system-design terms mixed together the way a real interview mixes them — the year did its job.

Throughput
How much work a system handles per unit time — rows/sec, requests/sec. The volume dimension.
Latency
How long one request takes end to end. The speed dimension; trades off against throughput and cost.
SLA
Service Level Agreement — the promised bound on availability or latency, and the consequence if you miss it.
Idempotency
Re-running a job yields the same result — no duplicates, no drift.
Partitioning
Splitting data by a key (date, region) so queries and jobs touch only the slices they need.
Grain
What a single fact-table row represents. Declared first, before any modeling.
Exactly-once
The processing guarantee that each record is applied one time — no loss, no duplication — even across retries.
Lakehouse
Warehouse-style tables and transactions on cheap lake storage — the modern default platform.
RAG
Retrieval-Augmented Generation — ground an LLM's answer in documents fetched at query time.
Eval
A repeatable, scored test of an AI system's output quality — your regression suite for non-determinism.
Agent
An LLM that plans and calls tools in a loop to accomplish a goal, not just answer once.
Guardrail
A control that constrains model inputs or outputs — safety, format, or policy — before they reach a user.

The finish line

Twenty years built the foundation — the instinct for how real systems fail, the scars from shipping under pressure, the domain depth in healthcare and PII that no bootcamp can hand anyone. These twelve months added the two things that foundation was missing: the modern vocabulary to name what you already knew, and the proof — two capstones and a public trail — that you can build it now. The gap between you and the title is no longer knowledge or evidence; it's just the walk into the room. Go take it.
